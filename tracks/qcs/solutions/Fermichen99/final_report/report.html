Challenge #113: Query-Efficient Sim-to-Real Quantum-Gate Calibration

https://github.com/QuantumBFS/quantum.harness/issues/113

The local control landscape has d²−1 curved directions, but model mismatch rotates their pulse-space orientation; model-informed search is therefore efficient when the basis is valid, while device-triggered relinearization is needed to retain the advantage at larger drift mismatch.

Challenge

What issue #113 asks, and the precise claim tested here.

A differentiable device model can produce a high-fidelity gate and exact pulse derivatives cheaply, but the physical device differs from that model and exposes only expensive, noisy measurements. Issue #113 asks whether the model Hessian identifies a low-dimensional pulse subspace that reduces closed-loop calibration cost, where that transfer fails as the model-device gap grows, and whether modest adaptation can recover the saving.

Physical statement under test

Near a model optimum, write Utarget†U(θ)=eiA. Gate fidelity is insensitive to the identity part of the Hermitian generator A, so only the traceless generators contribute curvature. Their dimension is d2-1: three for one qubit and fifteen for two qubits. This fixes the maximum local curvature rank, but it does not guarantee that a basis computed from an imperfect model keeps the correct orientation in pulse-parameter space.

L(θ)=1-1d|Tr(Utarget†U(θ))|,rank(∇θ2L)≤d2-1
Why this matters

Closed-loop evaluations consume real experiments and measurement shots, and derivative-free optimization becomes expensive as the number of pulse controls grows. A reliable reduced coordinate system can move most computation into the simulator while preserving the device's ability to correct model error.

Approach

A strict model/device split and an honest count of every black-box query.

Finite-shot numerical experiment

The model dynamics are H(t;θ)=H0+∑aua(t;θ)Ha. We first optimize θ* with model gradients, compute the Hessian or endpoint Jacobian at that pulse, and form an orthonormal basis Vk. Closed-loop candidates are restricted to θ=θ*+Vkc. The simulated device uses H0,true=H0+εΔH, with |ΔH|F=|H0|F, and returns only a binomial finite-shot estimate of scalar fidelity. The loop never sees the held-out Hamiltonian, its Jacobian, ε, a gradient, or exact fidelity.

Nominal basis
Leading eigenvectors of the Hessian at the nominal model optimum
Fixed ensemble basis
One precomputed basis from stacked, block-normalized endpoint Jacobians of the nominal model plus 15 declared perturbation models
Triggered adaptive
Start with nominal k=15; after a failed device-only certificate, recompute a k=20 ensemble-Jacobian basis at the device-selected pulse and continue
Held-out protocol
Five unseen drift seeds × three independent shot-noise seeds per ε; training and test seeds are disjoint
Measurement
65,536 shots per query; every query and shot counted
Derivative-free closed-loop optimizers

Five-point coordinate scan. For each reduced coordinate, the device is queried at offsets {-h,-h/2,0,h/2,h} with h=0.3. A one-dimensional quadratic is fitted; if its minimum lies inside the interval and is convex, that vertex is also queried, and the best measured point is retained. This transparent, fixed-cost pass is used for the single-qubit study and as the initial nominal k=15 stage of the adaptive two-qubit loop.

COBYQA. For the two-qubit fixed-space comparison and adaptive recovery stages, COBYQA constructs and updates a local quadratic model of the scalar loss inside a trust region, without device gradients. It is used after the coordinate scan because coupled motion among 20–30 reduced coordinates is better represented by a multivariate trust-region model than by another independent-axis pass.

Success criterion and statistics

A single favorable noisy observation is not accepted. The selected pulse is queried seven more times and succeeds only if the Wilson upper bound on infidelity, implemented with z=1.96, is at most 10-3. This is conservative relative to a nominal one-sided 95% bound. Failed runs are right-censored at budget+1: 501 queries in Figure 1 and 701 in Figure 2. Curves report the median and interquartile range across 15 runs; percentages are certified success rates.

Highlight

What is established, what is new, and what is not claimed.

Direct answer to issue #113

Yes: the model Hessian exposes the predicted d2-1 local geometry and, when its orientation transfers, it reduces black-box calibration cost substantially. The sweet spot is not a universal fixed k=d2-1 under arbitrary mismatch. The observed failure is a rotation of useful pulse directions, so the practical solution is to test transfer with device data and update the basis only when needed.

What is innovative

The key distinction is between intrinsic physical dimension and model-dependent pulse-space orientation. The method retains a strict black-box boundary and uses a statistically defined device certificate to decide when the inexpensive nominal search is sufficient and when model-side relinearization is warranted.

Significance of the output

The result turns the issue's qualitative warning about the sim-to-real gap into a measured design rule. Use the smallest physically motivated basis first; do not enlarge it merely because mismatch exists; trigger a new local basis when the observed device certificate says the current coordinates are inadequate.

Broader impact and limitation

This pattern can reduce experimental calibration effort whenever a differentiable surrogate is available but imperfect. The evidence here is deliberately limited to simulated drift-Hamiltonian mismatch, two gate sizes, and the declared finite-shot protocol. It does not establish performance on real hardware or under every form of device error.
